A standard "open" differential sends power to whichever wheel has the least resistance — which sounds harmless until one wheel lifts off the ground or loses traction entirely. At that point, all the engine's power goes to the wheel doing nothing, and the vehicle doesn't move.

## What a locking differential does

A locking differential forces both wheels on an axle to spin at the same speed, regardless of available traction. If one wheel is off the ground, the locked axle still sends power to the wheel that's touching the dirt.

## Front, rear, or both?

Most 4x4s with a locker fit it to the rear axle only. A rear locker solves the majority of low-traction situations — climbing loose or uneven terrain, crossing ruts, and traversing side slopes. A front locker adds capability in extreme technical terrain but makes steering noticeably heavier when engaged, since the front wheels are also doing the steering.

## When you don't need one

For most touring and moderate off-road driving, a good set of all-terrain tyres, correct tyre pressure, and sensible wheel placement solve more traction problems than a locker ever will. A locker is a tool for the last 10% of difficulty, not a substitute for driving technique.

## When it earns its keep

Rock crawling, steep loose climbs, and deep wheel-ruts are the scenarios where an open differential's weakness shows up fastest — and where a locked axle turns an impossible line into a manageable one.
