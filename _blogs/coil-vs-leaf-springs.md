If you've shopped for a ute or wagon, you've probably noticed some use coil springs and others still run leaf springs — usually at the rear. Neither is objectively better; they're different engineering trade-offs.

## How leaf springs work

A leaf spring is a stack of curved metal strips bolted at both ends to the chassis, with the axle mounted in the middle. As the wheel moves up and down, the whole leaf pack flexes. This design is simple, cheap to produce, and naturally resists axle wrap under load — which is why most one-tonne utes still use leaf springs at the rear, where high payload capacity matters.

## How coil springs work

A coil spring is a simple compression spring mounted between the chassis and a locating arm system (usually trailing arms and a Panhard rod, or a multi-link setup) that controls the axle's position. Because the spring only handles vertical load — location is a separate job — coil-sprung rear ends generally offer better articulation and a more compliant ride.

## The trade-off in practice

| Factor | Leaf Springs | Coil Springs |
|---|---|---|
| Payload capacity | Higher | Lower (typically) |
| Ride comfort unladen | Firmer | Smoother |
| Articulation | Lower | Higher |
| Complexity/cost | Lower | Higher |

## What this means when buying

If you regularly carry heavy loads — camper trays, water tanks, long-range fuel — a leaf-sprung rear will usually handle that weight with less sag over time. If ride comfort and off-road articulation matter more than outright payload, a coil-sprung rear (like the [Nissan Patrol]({{ "/vehicle/nissan-patrol-y62/" | relative_url }}) or [Mitsubishi Pajero Sport]({{ "/vehicle/mitsubishi-pajero-sport/" | relative_url }})) will generally feel better both on and off road.
